beat one

The envelope is public.

A Miden private note hides what it says — the amount, the asset, the recipient’s details are sealed. But the note still has to be filed somewhere the network can find it.

So it is filed under a label: a 32-bit tag, visible to anyone who asks. By default that label is derived straight from the receiver’s account number.

Watch the envelope cross. The label on its front is the only thing anyone can read.
beat two

Every payment to you looks the same.

Send six payments the ordinary way and all six carry one identical label, because the label is a function of your account number and your account number does not change.

The Watcher cannot tell who you are. It can tell that these six belong to one person — and that is enough to build a spending history out of nothing.

Its pile is one pile. Link rate 100%.
beat three

The dial.

Instead of filing under your own label, the library files each note under a different label from a pool that also contains yours. That is tag blinding.

The dial sets the pool width k — how many leading bits of your account number the label must still match. Low k is a wide pool: many labels to hide among, more noise for the receiver to fetch. High k is a narrow pool.

Drag the dial. Every label is now different, and the pile falls apart.
beat four

But the Librarian has a directory.

Account numbers are public. So an observer holding the list never needs your labels to repeat — given any label and the pool width, it can recompute which accounts could have produced it.

That list of maybe-accounts is the candidate set. Blinding does not defeat the Librarian. It makes the Librarian guess.

At the wide end the shortlist holds two or three names and one of them is gold.
beat five

A label names a group, not a person.

The whole tag space is 16,384 default-shaped labels. A pool width of k cuts that space into blocks of 2^(14−k). Your note lands in your block — always — because the block is fixed by the leading bits of your account number.

Blinding hides the label. It cannot hide the block. The only thing standing between a label and your name is how many other accounts happen to share your block.

Drag the dial and watch the dots leave the box. That box is a block. You are the gold dot.
beat six

The third face never moved.

The Watcher is lost. The Librarian is down to a shortlist it cannot shorten. Both were beaten by the dial.

The One Who Knows You does not have a dial to be beaten by. It holds your account number on a card, computes your pool from it, registers exactly those labels, and waits.

Thirty-six of thirty-six payments, at every setting of the dial.

Blinding makes you one face in a crowd. It does not make you invisible to somebody already looking at your face.
What is real here and what is not.
This page talks to nothing. No Miden SDK, no chain, no network — it is a preview of the demo, built so the idea could be seen before the demo was built.

Real: the account number at the centre of it (0x8e1bed3ae2cca241746ef1ca4bf1ec, recorded from an actual mock-chain run), the label arithmetic (top k bits of the 120-bit account number, shifted into the tag, with random fill below — the same code the library runs), the seven structural bits every Miden account number carries, and the measured figures in the table below, taken from Stage 6 on a mock chain with a 128-account directory and 72 notes.

Stand-in: the other 127 account numbers are generated, not recorded. They obey the same structural invariants as real Miden account numbers — seven fixed bit positions, uniform everywhere else — so the crowd behaves the way a real crowd behaves. The generator is seeded, so the page is the same every time you load it.

The seed was picked from 6,000 candidate crowds, and not for flattery: it reproduces the block sizes measured on the real 128 ids exactly (3, 3, 2, 1, 1, 1 at k = 7, 8, 9, 10, 12, 14) and matches the measured attribution at k=10 and k=14. Its k=7 attribution reads 43.8% against a measured 35.9% — which is inside the spread a crowd this size actually shows, and the reason both numbers are on screen at once.

The spread is the interesting part. Across 40 random crowds of 128, attribution at k=7 has a mean of 36.5% and a standard deviation of 4.8 points, ranging 25.8–47.7%. At k=14 the mean is 99.5%, sd 0.9. The closed form (1 − 2−k)127 is exactly that mean. So any single directory of this size is a sample, not a verdict — and the real demo will replay one recorded crowd and say which one.
