The argument · six cards, in order

beat one

The envelope is public.

A Miden private note → an encrypted payment record that only the accounts it names can open → sits on a chain anyone can read. The inside is sealed. The address on the outside is not.

Every note carries a tag → a small public number, 14 bits wide, printed on the outside of the note → and the node files notes by tag: hand over every note labelled 0x8e18. A watcher cannot open your note. They can see its label, how many notes wore that label, and when each one arrived.

Everything on this page follows from that one asymmetry: sealed inside, public outside.

The privacy was never in the seal. It is in the label.

beat two

Every payment to you looks the same.

The obvious tag to use is the one the recipient implies: the default tag → the tag computed from the top 14 bits of an account number →. Use it and every payment to that account wears an identical label. Six payments, one pile.

A watcher cannot name the pile’s owner from the label alone, and does not need to. Six notes sharing one label are one history: same recipient, six times over, on a timetable anyone can read.

Blinding → drawing the tag at random from a set the recipient has agreed to answer to, instead of using the one the account implies → breaks that. The six payments now wear six labels out of a pool → every tag one account listens for → of 128, and the pile splits into six, each indistinguishable from other traffic in the same pool.

Unblinded, its pile is one pile.

beat three

The dial.

How much to blind is one number. k → how many of the 14 tag bits the code still keeps from the account number → sets the pool at 214−k. At k = 14 everything is kept: the pool is one tag and blinding is switched off. At k = 7 seven bits are kept: the pool is 128 tags, and your note is one of 128 equally likely labels.

Every bit you give up doubles the crowd, and doubles what the recipient has to scan to find your note. In the library’s own run, the blinded receiver listened for 128 labels and recovered all twelve notes in four requests. The receiver listening for one label missed six of them.

There is no correct k. There is a crowd you are willing to stand in, and a scan you are willing to ask of whoever pays you.

Drag the dial.

beat four

But the Librarian has a directory.

The third figure at the counter holds something the first does not: the public list of account numbers. Attribution → the fraction of a crowd a watcher can name outright, from the label alone → is what that list buys.

At k = 7 the measured figure over 128 real account numbers was 35.9%. The arithmetic any crowd of 128 gives is 36.9% — close enough that the measurement is not doing work the formula was not already doing. At k = 14 it is 100%.

So blinding does not make an account unnameable. It makes naming you a question of how many bits you kept, and prints the answer in the pool size.

A shortlist is not a suspicion. It is arithmetic.

beat five

A label names a group, not a person.

Here is the ceiling. Blinding hides the label, not the block → the accounts whose numbers produce the same tag: everyone a watcher has to consider together →.

At k = 7 the block holding this film’s account measured three accounts; a crowd of 128 gives 2.0 on average. You are hidden inside a group of three. That is not anonymity. It is company.

Narrow the pool and the company thins. By k = 10 the block is one account, and the label points at a single number again — with no scan anybody had to widen.

One of a few. Never one of none.

beat six

The third face never moved.

One watcher here is untouched by any of it. The targeted observer → a party who already holds your account number and watches for exactly what it implies → read 36 of 36 at every width, legacy included. Nothing on this page moves that number, and no setting of the dial will.

Blinding is metadata minimisation → shrinking what the outside of the envelope says, not what the inside holds → against strangers reading a public chain. It is not a defence against a party who was told your number.

The film stops here instead of on a win, because the number is true.

Nothing here stops someone who already knows you.

Footnotes · what is real, what is not, and how much this varies

Real. The account number in this film, 0x8e1bed3ae2cca241746ef1ca4bf1ec, is a real testnet account id. Its default tag, the pool arithmetic and the closed forms behind every small line are the library’s own code (src/envelope). The structural facts the design leans on — a 120-bit id whose bit 60 carries the storage mode, top bits uniform to within coin-flip noise, and independent rather than merely uniform — were measured across 519 real account ids and are logged as MIDEN-013 and MIDEN-015. Every large figure in the readings was measured in Stage 6 over 128 real account ids.

Stand-in. The other 127 accounts in the crowd are generated, not real. They still behave like real ones because the only property the arithmetic uses is that the top 14 bits are uniform and independent, which is exactly what MIDEN-015 tested on real directories. The faces are placeholder line drawings; commissioned art replaces them once the direction is agreed. This page sends and receives nothing — no chain, no mock, no network. The library running against a simulated chain, with its receipts on screen, is demo/index.html.

The spread. A crowd of 128 is a sample. Across generated crowds at k = 7 attribution averaged 36.5% with a standard deviation of 4.8 points, so any single directory — the measured one included — can sit several points off the closed form. Read the small model line as the centre, not as the answer. At k = 14 there is almost nothing left to vary: mean 99.5%.
